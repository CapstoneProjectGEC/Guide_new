Beluru, also written Belur, is a small town in Hassan district, Karnataka, known for temples and monuments that show Hoysala artistic and architectural skill. It was once the Hoysala capital and was then called Velapuri. The page says the Hoysalas ruled most of South India from the 10th to the 14th century from two capitals at different times: Beluru (Velapuri) and Halebeedu (Dwarasamudra).

According to the page, King Vishnuvardhana built the Chennakeshava Temple in 1117 CE to mark his victory over the Cholas, and completing it took more than a century. The old capital Velapuri was founded on the banks of the Yagachi River.

The Sowmyanayaki temple is described as Hoysala-period and renovated under Vijayanagara rule in 1387. The Ranganayaki (Andal) temple is said, per an ASI plaque outside it, to have been built by the Vijayanagara kings from material of ruined nearby Hoysala-period temples.

**Q:** What was Belur called in Hoysala times?  
**A:** Velapuri (ExploreKarnataka).

**Q:** Who built the Chennakeshava Temple and when?  
**A:** King Vishnuvardhana, in 1117 CE, to mark his victory over the Cholas, according to ExploreKarnataka.

**Q:** What were the two Hoysala capitals?  
**A:** Beluru (Velapuri) and Halebeedu (Dwarasamudra).

**Q:** How long did the temple take to build?  
**A:** ExploreKarnataka says more than a century; Temples.bio says 103 years.

The Chennakeshava Temple (also Keshava Temple or Belur Temple) is dedicated to Vishnu and is called one of the finest examples of Hoysala architecture. It is decorated with fine soapstone carvings showing scenes from the Hindu epics and Puranas. The page also mentions a 100-feet high tower, a grand entrance gate and a six-feet tall Vishnu idol in the sanctum.

The author remarks that it is hard to believe such fine filigree could be made in stone and that the skill of the Hoysala artisans is beyond words.

Photographs on the page document: the main entrance, whose door jamb shows Ugra Narasimha mounted on Garuda slaying the demon Hiranyakashyapa (IMG-0002); the Hoysala emblem, a warrior hunting a lion (IMG-0003, IMG-0004); an interior array of high-precision circular pillars (IMG-0006); carved dwara palakas (door guardians) at the inner sanctum (IMG-0007, IMG-0016); stone-carved maidens, one holding a staff with a skull and wearing a skull-decorated crown (IMG-0008, IMG-0009) and others playing drums and dancing (IMG-0010 to IMG-0013); and Ugra Narasimha slaying Hiranyakashyapa and garlanding the intestines, seen both on the outer wall and on an interior ceiling (IMG-0014, IMG-0015).

Temples.bio describes the temple as standing on a star-shaped platform in black soapstone, with every inch carved. Architecture highlights listed: the 42 Madanika bracket figures, the rotating pillar, and the monolithic lotus ceiling of the Navaranga. The 42 Madanika figures are called the finest stone figurative carvings in South Asia.

Sacred objects listed: the Darpana Sundari (Lady with Mirror) carving on the outer wall and the queen Shanthala's Garuda pillar inscription. Devotee practices include walking around the platform to observe more than 10,000 carvings and hiring a guide to read the Puranic narratives.

Special tradition listed: a 'gravity-defying pillar in the courtyard' under which a sheet of paper can be passed because it rests on its own centre of mass.
